Democrat Scott Colom wins Mississippi Senate seat by 4,669 votes

Democrat Scott Colom defeated Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in Mississippi's U.S. Senate race, the Associated Press and NBC News projected Wednesday, November 11, eight days after Election Day. In the AP's unofficial count, with about 99% of the vote in, Colom had 473,336 votes (49.2%) to Hyde-Smith's 468,667 (48.7%), a margin of 4,669; independent Ty Pinkins had 20,234 (2.1%). The AP said it called the race at 5:58 p.m. Eastern after Jackson County reported another 2,173 votes, and estimated fewer than 12,000 votes remained for county officials to review. Colom led by 43 votes after the final election-night update and his lead grew as counties processed absentee and affidavit (provisional) ballots (AP). Colom, 43, the district attorney for Mississippi's 16th Circuit Court District, will be the first Democrat Mississippi has elected to the Senate since 1982 and the state's first Black U.S. senator since Reconstruction (NBC News, AP). Hyde-Smith called Colom to concede and said in a video message, "While this is not the outcome we worked for or hoped for, I respect the decision of Mississippi voters" (NBC News). NBC News reported that Colom is the eighth Democrat to flip a Republican-held Senate seat this year and that Democrats will hold at least 55 Senate seats next year, with only Alaska's race uncalled. Hyde-Smith, appointed in 2018 and elected to a full term in 2020, had blocked Colom's nomination to a federal judgeship during the Biden administration (NBC News; Ballotpedia).

Quick facts

  • Result (AP unofficial count): Colom 473,336 (49.2%), Hyde-Smith 468,667 (48.7%), Pinkins 20,234 (2.1%)
  • Margin: 4,669 votes; AP call Wednesday, Nov. 11, 5:58 p.m. ET
  • First Democrat elected to the Senate from Mississippi since 1982; first Black senator from the state since Reconstruction
  • Hyde-Smith conceded by phone and in a video message
  • Democrats: 8 Senate seats flipped this year; at least 55 seats next year (NBC News); Alaska uncalled
  • Trump carried Mississippi by 23 points in 2024 (NBC News)

Not yet confirmed

  • Final certified totals; the figures above are unofficial and may change as counties finish reviewing ballots
  • The outcome of Alaska's Senate race, which determines whether Democrats hold 55 or 56 seats

What happens next

  • Mississippi counties certify their results Friday, November 13, and the secretary of state declares the statewide result December 3, according to an account of the AP's race call. Mississippi has no automatic recount; any challenge would go through the courts. The new Senate term begins January 3, 2027.

Nvidia board meets on whether to oust CEO Jensen Huang

Nvidia's board met Wednesday evening to address CEO Jensen Huang's future at the company, two sources familiar with the matter told NBC News; CNBC also confirmed the meeting, and Reuters reported it citing a source. As of Thursday morning, no decision had been announced (CNBC, The Next Web citing Reuters). The meeting followed a New York Times report Wednesday, citing five people who worked at Nvidia, that Huang had a relationship with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, who reported to him. NBC News said it had not confirmed the Times report. On Sunday, Huang told employees he had been "unfaithful to his wife," after reports about his relationship with Alice Huang, a Megaspeed executive whom NBC News reported U.S. intelligence agencies had determined was a Chinese intelligence operative. China's Foreign Ministry called the allegations "purely unsubstantiated." Nvidia shares fell more than 8% in after-hours trading Wednesday (The Guardian) and were down 6.9% in premarket trading at 6:23 a.m. Eastern Thursday (CNBC). The board said Sunday it had confidence in Huang; its independent directors have hired the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to investigate (The Guardian). Under Nvidia's bylaws, a majority of its 11 directors can remove the CEO "with or without cause." Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993. Late Wednesday, Sens. Elizabeth Warren and Chris Coons asked the Commerce Department, the State Department and the White House to preserve records on Nvidia chip exports to China (NBC News). Nvidia did not respond to requests for comment.

Quick facts

  • Board meeting: Wednesday evening; no announced outcome as of Thursday morning
  • New report: NYT says Huang had a relationship with a direct report, VP of healthcare Kimberly Powell (not independently confirmed by NBC News)
  • Shares: down more than 8% after hours Wednesday; down 6.9% premarket Thursday (as of 6:23 a.m. ET)
  • Board: 11 directors, all but Huang considered independent; a majority can remove the CEO
  • Bernstein cut its share target to $200 from $400 (CNBC)
  • This follows Briefed's Nov. 9 and Nov. 10 reports on the first relationship and the board's investigation

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether the board will remove Huang, and who would lead the company if it does
  • The Times report about Powell; Huang, Powell and Nvidia did not comment
  • Whether Huang knew of any intelligence link; Huang denied knowledge, according to President Trump

What happens next

  • Nvidia is scheduled to report quarterly results Tuesday, November 17. The Wachtell investigation is ongoing.

Brooklyn building collapse kills eight, injures 17

A three-story building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue, at the corner of Bedford Avenue in Flatbush, Brooklyn, collapsed just after 4 p.m. Wednesday into an excavation site next door at 2625 Bedford Avenue. As of about 7:20 a.m. Thursday, the FDNY says eight people have died and 17 were injured (ABC7 New York). Two of the dead died at the hospital, and four victims found in the rubble early Thursday are believed to be construction workers, ABC7 reported. Earlier Thursday the FDNY reported six dead and 19 injured (CBS News New York, NY1). Seven children were among those treated, and one firefighter had minor injuries. The building had a store at street level and apartments upstairs; the side of the neighboring building at 1323 Flatbush Avenue was torn off (NYPD via CBS News New York). People from both the building and the construction site were trapped. FDNY Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore said officials do not know how many people may still be in the collapse zone, and crews are using drones, buckets, vacuum trucks and dogs to search the rubble. The Buildings Department says the developers of the adjacent site had an active permit for excavation and foundation work and has since issued a full stop-work order (ABC7). City records show the department issued a violation in April to a demolition contractor at that lot for demolishing a building despite a stop-work order; the violation is listed as resolved (CBS News New York). It is not known whether work at the site caused the collapse. The Buildings Department and law enforcement are investigating, and the Brooklyn district attorney's office is examining whether any criminal conduct contributed.

Quick facts

  • Where: 1321 Flatbush Ave., at Bedford Ave., Flatbush, Brooklyn
  • When: just after 4 p.m. Wednesday, Nov. 11
  • Toll (FDNY, about 7:20 a.m. Thursday, via ABC7): 8 dead, 17 injured; two of the dead died at the hospital
  • Seven children were among those treated; one firefighter had minor injuries
  • More than 250 firefighters and EMS personnel responded
  • Families seeking information: call 311, or 212-639-9675 from outside New York City

Not yet confirmed

  • How many people may still be missing
  • How many of the injured remain in critical condition; ABC7's update gives both "four" and "at least two"
  • The cause of the collapse, including any role of the excavation next door
  • The names and ages of the victims

What happens next

  • Officials say the search will continue for several days. A service center for affected residents opens at 9 a.m. Thursday at the Flatbush YMCA (NY1).

DHS says ICE deported a record 483,200 people in FY2026

These are the Department of Homeland Security's own figures, obtained by CBS News; they have not been independently audited. DHS says Immigration and Customs Enforcement deported 483,200 people in fiscal year 2026, which ended September 30, above its previous record of 442,640 in fiscal 2025, and made more than 491,000 arrests, including nearly 475,000 administrative (civil) arrests. DHS says the deportation total is about 18% above the 409,849 removals in fiscal 2012 and about 78% above the 271,484 in fiscal 2024. "Fiscal Year 2026 saw the highest number of ICE deportations and arrests for any year in ICE's history," DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said in a statement. CBS News noted that ICE's figures exclude actions by Customs and Border Protection, and that broader governmentwide deportation measures were higher in parts of the early 2000s. An analysis published Wednesday by Syracuse University's Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse (TRAC), based on case-by-case DHS records, found formal removals made up 63% of deportations in July and August 2026; voluntary departures were 31% and voluntary returns 6%. TRAC also found monthly removals fell to 26,908 in July 2026 from 30,893 in February 2025. The Migration Policy Institute found about 20% of roughly 980,000 ICE and CBP deportations from January 2025 to July 2026 involved someone with a criminal conviction (via CBS News).

Quick facts

  • DHS claim: 483,200 ICE deportations in FY2026 (FY2025: 442,640)
  • DHS claim: 491,000+ ICE arrests, including about 475,000 administrative arrests; monthly high of 50,925 in August
  • TRAC: formal removals were 63% of deportations in July-August 2026
  • MPI: about 20% of ICE and CBP deportations since January 2025 involved a criminal conviction
  • CBS News exit polling: 58% of midterm voters said enforcement had gone too far

Not yet confirmed

  • The DHS totals; the department's full data release was not posted on dhs.gov when this was written, and the counts include voluntary departures and returns
  • How DHS defines and counts a deportation versus a formal removal

What happens next

  • Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress in January; some have said they want more oversight of immigration enforcement. ICE has about $75 billion in additional funding from the July 2025 tax and spending law (CBS News).

USDA proposes SNAP rule it says would cut 6.3 million people

The Agriculture Department published a proposed rule in the Federal Register on November 10 that would narrow "categorical eligibility" for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). Under current rules, households that receive cash or non-cash benefits from programs funded by Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) can qualify for SNAP automatically. The proposal would limit that to households that receive TANF "assistance" as defined in federal regulation, so non-cash services such as child care or job training would no longer confer SNAP eligibility (Federal Register; CBS News). USDA estimates about 16.9% of participating households, 3.2 million households containing 6.3 million people, would no longer receive SNAP in fiscal 2029, the first year the rule would take full effect, and that federal SNAP benefit spending would fall by about $44.6 billion from fiscal 2027 to 2031. The agency estimates nearly 1.8 million children would lose access to free school meals (CBS News). A USDA spokesperson said broad-based categorical eligibility "has bloated the welfare rolls for decades." The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities and the Food Research and Action Center criticized the proposal, and Rep. Angie Craig of Minnesota, the top Democrat on the House Agriculture Committee, called it "heartless and cruel" (CBS News). A similar 2019 proposal was withdrawn in 2021.

Quick facts

  • Rule: "Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program: Reforming Categorical Eligibility," FR Doc. 2026-22936
  • USDA estimate: 3.2 million households, 6.3 million people off SNAP in FY2029
  • USDA estimate: about $44.6 billion less in federal SNAP benefits, FY2027-2031
  • More than 40 states use broad-based categorical eligibility (CBS News)
  • Comment deadline: December 10, 2026

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether USDA will change the rule after public comments, and when a final rule would be issued
  • Advocacy groups say the impact could be larger than USDA's estimate

What happens next

  • Public comments are open through December 10 on Regulations.gov (docket FNA-2026-0661). USDA would then review comments before any final rule.

U.S. · Washington, DCReported only

Massie petition for second Epstein files bill reaches 218

A discharge petition from Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Ky., to force a House vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act II reached the 218 signatures needed on Wednesday, November 11 (ABC News, Axios). All 214 House Democrats signed, along with four Republicans: Massie and Reps. Nancy Mace and Ralph Norman of South Carolina and Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania. Reps. Adam Smith and John Garamendi were the final signers (The Hill, Axios). The bill would let state attorneys general, Epstein's victims and members of Congress sue the U.S. attorney general over Epstein-related records the Justice Department withholds or redacts without justification (ABC News). Congress passed the first Epstein Files Transparency Act in November 2025 after an earlier Massie discharge petition; the House vote was 427-1, and President Trump signed it. The Justice Department missed that law's December 19, 2025, deadline and said in January it had released nearly 3.5 million pages (Axios). The White House did not answer Axios's questions about the new bill.

Quick facts

  • Signatures: 218 (214 Democrats, 4 Republicans)
  • Republican signers: Massie, Mace, Norman, Fitzpatrick
  • Bill: lets states, victims and members of Congress sue over withheld or redacted Epstein records
  • Discharge petitions have succeeded eight times in the 119th Congress (ABC News)

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether the bill would pass the House, or get a Senate vote before the new Congress begins in January (Axios)

What happens next

  • The petition must sit on the House calendar for seven legislative days; a signer can then call it up, and the speaker has two legislative days to schedule it. Floor action would come after the Thanksgiving recess, potentially as early as December 2 or 3 (ABC News).
