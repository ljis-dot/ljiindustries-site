St. Johns River near Cocoa hits a 73-year flood record of 18.8 feet

The St. Johns River near Cocoa, Florida, was at about 18.75 feet on Friday and was forecast to crest around 18.8 feet on Saturday, above the 1953 record of about 17.6 feet, according to the St. Johns River Water Management District, as reported by WESH, and National Weather Service gauge data. WESH said rain totals surpassed 28 inches in some areas of the watershed. The city of Rockledge still had a voluntary evacuation in effect on Saturday, and the National Guard was using high-water vehicles to reach flooded neighborhoods, ClickOrlando reported. The water-management district said the river near Cocoa could remain at or above moderate flood stage for two to three months, through Thanksgiving and into winter. AccuWeather said major flooding near Cocoa could linger through December and that at least minor flooding is likely into January 2027. The rain had already ended in Brevard County; the remaining threat is the slow-rising river, not further downpours. The Associated Press reported historic flooding along Florida's Atlantic coast, with officials urging residents in vulnerable neighborhoods to be ready to leave, and dozens of rescues earlier in the week.

Quick facts

  • Forecast crest near Cocoa: about 18.8 feet on Saturday, Nov. 21
  • Previous record: about 17.6 feet in 1953 (WESH; AccuWeather cited 17.55 feet on Oct. 11, 1953)
  • Rain totals surpassed 28 inches in some areas (WESH)
  • Rockledge: voluntary evacuation still in effect; National Guard high-water vehicles
  • Time at or above moderate flood stage near Cocoa: two to three months (SJRWMD)
  • The district said it was storing about 60 billion gallons that would otherwise flow downstream
  • Pet-friendly shelter: Walter Butler Community Center, Cocoa

Not yet confirmed

  • How high the river ultimately crests at gauges downstream, including Lake Harney, Sanford, DeLand and Astor
  • How long individual homes will remain flooded; AccuWeather's "into 2027" line is a forecast of lingering minor flooding, not of homes staying underwater until then
  • Whether any additional rain later this week changes the outlook

What happens next

  • The river near Cocoa was expected to hold near 18.8 feet this weekend
  • Downstream crests are forecast over the coming week to 10 days
  • Thanksgiving travel in east Central Florida may still face flooded roads

Thanksgiving dinner for 10 rises 6%, to $58.63, the first increase since 2022

The American Farm Bureau Federation's 41st annual Thanksgiving dinner survey found that a classic dinner for 10 now costs $58.63, or $5.86 a person, up 6% from $55.18 last year, the first increase since 2022, the Farm Bureau, the Associated Press, Axios and The Washington Post reported. A 16-pound frozen turkey averaged $23.68, up $2.38, or 10%. The meal is still about 8% below the 2022 peak of $64.05, and above the 2019 pre-pandemic cost of $48.91. Regional averages for 10 people were $61.57 in the Northeast, $58.74 in the West, $56.89 in the Midwest and $54.33 in the South. Cranberries were up about 9%. Volunteer shoppers checked prices in the first week of November, possibly before later promotions; some found turkey as low as 69 cents a pound. A separate Wells Fargo Agri-Food Institute report, using different menus and 52-week retail data through September, found Thanksgiving foods 0.4% cheaper than a year earlier.

Quick facts

  • $58.63 for 10 people ($5.86 each), up 6% from $55.18
  • 16-pound frozen turkey: $23.68, up 10%
  • Still about 8% below the 2022 record of $64.05
  • Northeast $61.57; West $58.74; Midwest $56.89; South $54.33
  • Related, not the hook: AAA's national average for regular gasoline was $3.6594 as of Saturday, Nov. 21, down from $3.78 earlier this week, still above the 2022 Thanksgiving-Day record of $3.58

Not yet confirmed

  • What shoppers will actually pay after last-week promotions, coupons and store meal baskets (Axios noted Walmart's about-$35 basket for 10 and an Aldi meal tested at $37.38)
  • Whether the Wells Fargo and Farm Bureau figures can be directly compared; they use different methods

What happens next

  • Thanksgiving is Thursday, Nov. 26
  • Many grocery promotions run through the holiday week

Dow and S&P 500 close in correction; Nasdaq finishes lower for the year

U.S. stocks sold off again Friday as the AI trade unwound. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 949.73 points, or 1.93%, to 48,147.33, the S&P 500 fell 3.7% to 6,910.39, and both closed more than 10% below their record closes, entering correction territory, CNBC, the Associated Press and Yahoo Finance reported. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.28% to 22,617.73 and finished 2.7% lower for 2026. CNBC said the Nasdaq-100 fell 5.6% and entered a bear market on an intraday basis, 20% below its recent high. Nvidia fell about 14%, AMD more than 13% and Micron almost 14%. Anthropic fell 8.5% in its second session, after a 17% jump on Thursday's debut, but remained above its $83 offer price. Reuters, citing sources, reported that SEC staff are examining possible accounting issues at Nvidia; CNBC said that report added to Friday's selling. The SEC has not announced a formal public action in the coverage reviewed for this briefing. For the week, the Dow fell 8%, its worst week since March 2020; the Nasdaq was down about 10%, its largest one-week drop in more than six years; and the S&P 500 fell 8.6%, its worst week since April 2025. Yahoo Finance said gold futures dropped nearly 4% and bitcoin fell below $59,000, its lowest since July.

Quick facts

  • Dow: 48,147.33, down 949.73 points (−1.93%)
  • S&P 500: 6,910.39 (−3.7%); Nasdaq: 22,617.73 (−5.28%), down 2.7% year to date
  • Both the Dow and S&P 500 closed more than 10% below their record closes
  • Nvidia about −14%; Anthropic −8.5% in session two, still above $83
  • Week: Dow −8% (worst since March 2020); Nasdaq about −10%; S&P 500 −8.6%

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether the SEC inquiry becomes a formal public enforcement action; Reuters attributed the accounting review to sources
  • Where markets open on Monday
  • Reports on gold and bitcoin were not the focus of this briefing and were not independently rechecked beyond Yahoo Finance

What happens next

  • U.S. markets are closed Saturday and Sunday; the next regular session is Monday
  • The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, is due next week
  • The Fed's next rate decision is at its December meeting

Russia violates Polish airspace; a strike on Kryvyi Rih kills 2 and wounds 60

Polish officials said a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile and two other objects entered Polish airspace early Friday. Defence Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz said the missile hit a rural area at Nowy Lipowiec, about 31 miles (50 kilometers) from the Ukrainian border, around 6:30 a.m. local time, with no injuries reported. Another suspected missile went down near Rudnik nad Sanem, and a jet-powered drone crashed near Mołodycz, he said. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the incursion did not appear to be an accident and noted nearby Koalicja 2026 military exercises. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte condemned "grave violations of Polish airspace," said NATO jets were scrambled, and was due in Warsaw on Saturday. Russia had made no comment on the Poland incursion in BBC and Reuters coverage. Overnight, a Russian strike on the center of President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's hometown, Kryvyi Rih, killed a 7-year-old boy and a 20-year-old woman and wounded 60 people, including six children, local officials told the Associated Press on Saturday.

Quick facts

  • Poland: three objects, probably two missiles and a drone, officials said; no injuries
  • Kh-101 struck Nowy Lipowiec, about 31 miles from the Ukrainian border
  • NATO condemned the violations and said jets were scrambled
  • Kryvyi Rih: 2 killed (a 7-year-old boy and a 20-year-old woman) and 60 wounded, including six children (AP, Saturday)
  • Earlier BBC injury counts were lower; later local tallies moved as officials updated figures

Not yet confirmed

  • Russia's account of the Poland incursion; Moscow had not commented in the coverage reviewed
  • Tusk's statement that the objects' landing near the exercises was "no coincidence" is his claim
  • Final casualty figures in Kryvyi Rih may still change

What happens next

  • Rutte was due in Warsaw on Saturday
  • Poland said it would ask NATO for additional support
  • The war in Ukraine continues, with further Russian strikes reported overnight

Trump threatens a tax on overseas AI data centers; a second judge blocks his arch

President Trump said Friday that U.S. companies that build artificial intelligence data centers abroad will face "a tremendous tax." He gave no rate, start date or legal basis, and told reporters he had not yet informed his own officials, the Associated Press, Axios and CNBC reported. Axios and other coverage noted that Congress, not the president, has the constitutional power to impose a federal tax, so the remarks describe a threat, not an enacted policy. Also on Friday, U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema in Alexandria, Virginia, issued a preliminary injunction blocking the administration from starting construction of a proposed 250-foot memorial arch at Memorial Circle, the second federal court order against the project this week after Judge Tanya Chutkan in Washington extended a temporary block through Dec. 3. Brinkema, ruling from the bench, called the plans "such a rushed job" and said the project lacked congressional approval, Courthouse News reported. A Justice Department spokesman said the government will appeal.

Quick facts

  • Trump: companies "can" build data centers abroad, "but we're going to put a tremendous tax on them"
  • He said he had not yet told "his people"
  • No rate, statute or start date was named
  • Planned arch: 250 feet at Memorial Circle; second injunction this week
  • Brinkema: "this is a project without congressional approval"
  • DOJ said it will appeal

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether any tax or penalty is actually proposed to Congress or imposed by executive action
  • The outcome of the government's planned appeal
  • County filings include additional allegations about the arch's design and use; those are the county's claims, not findings of the court, except as the judge addressed them

What happens next

  • The injunctions keep construction on hold while the lawsuits proceed
  • Chutkan's order runs through Dec. 3; Brinkema's preliminary injunction extends the halt further
  • No tax legislation had been introduced as of Saturday morning

Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth, about 16.1 billion miles

NASA said Voyager 1, launched in 1977, reached one light-day from Earth — about 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometers — at 5:16 a.m. Eastern on Wednesday, Nov. 18, becoming the first human-made object to travel so far that a radio signal now takes 24 hours to arrive. The mission confirmed the milestone when the spacecraft's carrier signal reached Earth 24 hours later, on Thursday, NASA said in a Nov. 19 release, also reported by the Associated Press and Space.com. Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012 and is traveling about 38,000 mph. Its twin, Voyager 2, is not expected to reach one light-day until 2035. NASA said the team has not received science and engineering data from Voyager 1 since Nov. 14. The spacecraft is still sending a carrier signal, which engineers used with trajectory calculations to confirm the milestone, and they continue to analyze the data problem. NASA said recent power-saving changes are expected to keep the current science instruments running until at least the 50th launch anniversary next summer.

Quick facts

  • Milestone: 5:16 a.m. ET, Wednesday, Nov. 18, 2026
  • Distance: about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers)
  • One-way radio time: 24 hours; a command-and-response cycle is two days
  • Science and engineering data have not come in since Nov. 14; the carrier signal continues
  • Voyager 2 is expected to reach one light-day in 2035

Not yet confirmed

  • When, or whether, the science and engineering data stream will be restored
  • How long the 1977 probes will keep communicating as their power supply declines

What happens next

  • Engineers continue to diagnose the data issue
  • The mission's 50th launch anniversary is next summer (2027)
