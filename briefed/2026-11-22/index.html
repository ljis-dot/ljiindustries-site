'El Chapo Isidro,' an FBI Ten Most Wanted fugitive, is captured in Mexico with U.S. help

Mexican special forces captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as "El Chapo Isidro," a Sinaloa Cartel leader and one of the FBI's Ten Most Wanted fugitives, in Guasave, Sinaloa, on Friday, Nov. 20, the U.S. Department of Justice, NPR (from the Associated Press), and ABC News reported. The Justice Department said a U.S. Homeland Security Task Force investigation and U.S. intelligence supported the operation. Meza Flores faced a $5 million U.S. reward and is under indictment in Washington, D.C., on narco-terrorism and fentanyl-trafficking charges. Mexico's Defense Ministry said a firefight during the operation left at least 11 dead, including 4 Mexican soldiers and 7 alleged gunmen. FBI Director Kash Patel said he is the 11th Ten Most Wanted fugitive captured under the Trump administration, the Justice Department relayed. Extradition has not been announced; the United States wants him extradited. If convicted in the U.S., he would face the death penalty or mandatory life, the Justice Department said.

Quick facts

  • Captured Friday, Nov. 20, in Guasave, Sinaloa, with U.S. help (DOJ)
  • One of the FBI's Ten Most Wanted; $5 million U.S. reward
  • At least 11 dead in the firefight, including 4 Mexican soldiers (Mexico's Defense Ministry)
  • FBI Director Kash Patel: the 11th Ten Most Wanted capture under this administration (via DOJ)
  • Under indictment in Washington, D.C., on narco-terrorism and fentanyl-trafficking charges

Not yet confirmed

  • Extradition has not been announced
  • A full official accounting of everyone killed or wounded in the firefight, beyond the Defense Ministry's at-least-11 figure
  • Who will take over Meza Flores's faction, if anyone

What happens next

  • The United States wants him extradited to face the D.C. indictment
  • Mexican authorities continue to hold him pending any formal extradition request

San Francisco's tide is forecast at 8.8 feet Wednesday, above the 1983 record, as storms threaten Thanksgiving travel

Forecasters expect king tides plus storm surge in San Francisco to peak around 8.8 feet at 10:49 a.m. PT on Wednesday, Nov. 25, above the 8.66-foot record of Jan. 27, 1983, according to coverage from Weather.com, the Associated Press via ABC News, and NBC Bay Area. That 8.8-foot figure is a forecast, including storm surge. NOAA's astronomical-only tide prediction for that tide at station 9414290 is 7.03 feet, so the rest is forecast storm surge. Mayor Daniel Lurie urged residents to stock up over the weekend and avoid nonessential travel from Monday afternoon through Wednesday. A National Weather Service coastal flood warning covers the Bay Area from 6 a.m. Monday to 4 p.m. Thursday; the AP said up to 2 feet of flooding is likely in low-lying shoreline areas. Separately, a coastal storm is bringing rain and gusty winds up the East Coast today, Sunday, with possible afternoon flight delays at Northeast hubs. Weather.com said Monday looks calmer, then storms return to both coasts from Tuesday night, including a potential major Northeast storm on Thanksgiving Day and an atmospheric river on the West Coast Thursday–Friday. These are forecasts several days out; a Thanksgiving Day Northeast storm is possible, not certain.

Quick facts

  • Forecast SF peak: about 8.8 feet (MLLW) Wednesday, Nov. 25, at 10:49 a.m. PT
  • Prior record: 8.66 feet on Jan. 27, 1983
  • NOAA astronomical-only prediction for that tide: 7.03 feet (the rest is forecast storm surge)
  • Mayor Lurie: skip nonessential travel Monday afternoon through Wednesday
  • East Coast storm is hitting today, Sunday; a major Northeast storm is possible on Thanksgiving

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether Wednesday's tide actually tops the 1983 record (this is a forecast)
  • Whether a major Northeast storm materializes on Thanksgiving Day
  • How many flights, if any, are delayed or canceled; delays are possible, not promised
  • How high water gets in any given neighborhood

What happens next

  • Bay Area coastal flood warning: 6 a.m. Monday through 4 p.m. Thursday
  • Thanksgiving is Thursday, Nov. 26
  • The St. Johns River near Cocoa, Florida, covered in Saturday's briefing, remains high after its record crest

U.S. · USAReported only

FBI is investigating an apparent hack of the National Crime Information Center

The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of the National Crime Information Center, the nationwide database federal, state and local police use for warrants, criminal histories and background checks, according to two people briefed on the matter, NBC News, the BBC, and Reuters (via CNBC) reported, following a CNN report Friday evening. It is not known who did it or whether data was stolen or changed. The FBI has not commented in the coverage reviewed for this briefing. Asked about it on Fox News Saturday, President Trump said he had "just heard" and "it's not good. I don't like that." This would be the second intrusion into an FBI system in recent months; in September, the ShinyHunters group claimed to have taken data on about 38,000 FBI staff through a jobs portal, the BBC reported. Separately, NBC reported that hackers breached a USDA system used to pay farmers this week, and that White House National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross is leaving; the BBC said President Trump fired him. Those side developments were not the focus of this briefing.

Quick facts

  • Target: the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), used for warrants, criminal histories and background checks
  • Reporting rests on people briefed on the matter; the FBI has not commented
  • Unknown: who did it, and whether data was taken or changed
  • President Trump, Saturday: he had "just heard" and "it's not good"

Not yet confirmed

  • The FBI has not confirmed the investigation or any loss or alteration of data
  • Who is responsible
  • Whether any warrants, records or background-check data were actually accessed or changed
  • How the USDA farm-payment and Cairncross personnel reports relate, if at all, to the NCIC incident

What happens next

  • Further official comment from the FBI or the Justice Department, if it comes
  • Police departments that rely on NCIC may look for guidance on whether to treat records as reliable

U.S. · WashingtonSourced

Trump order requires federal contractors to clear congressional requests with the White House

President Trump signed Executive Order 14438 late Friday, Nov. 20. It requires every federal contractor and subcontractor to notify the White House and its contracting agency right away of any congressional request about its work with the executive branch, and to follow White House instructions to withhold anything deemed covered by executive privilege, according to the White House text of the order and coverage from CNBC and Reuters. Companies that do not comply could have contracts terminated or be suspended or debarred. The White House says the order protects confidential and national-security information and avoids duplicative litigation. Reuters has noted it could face legal challenges if contractors are told to withhold subpoenaed material; no lawsuit had been filed in the coverage reviewed for this briefing. The order comes weeks before Democrats take control of both chambers in January, and follows a White House Counsel directive requiring approval before agency employees testify. Incoming Oversight chair Rep. Robert Garcia has asked 12 Trump allies and relatives, including Stephen Miller and JD Vance, to preserve documents, Politico reported.

Quick facts

  • Executive Order 14438, dated Nov. 20, 2026
  • Contractors and subcontractors must notify the White House of congressional requests about executive-branch work
  • They must follow White House instructions to withhold material it deems covered by executive privilege
  • Enforcement can include contract termination, suspension or debarment
  • White House rationale: protect confidential and national-security information and avoid duplicative litigation

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether, and how, the order will be challenged in court; legal challenges have been discussed, not filed in the coverage reviewed
  • How agencies and contractors will apply "executive privilege" in practice
  • Whether Congress will change its oversight requests in response

What happens next

  • Democrats are set to take both chambers in January
  • Contractors must decide how to handle incoming congressional requests under the new order

U.S. · VirginiaSourced

A Virginia child dies with measles, the state's first measles-associated death since 1978

The Virginia Department of Health said Friday, Nov. 20, that a young child, age 0 to 4, who tested positive for measles has died. VDH called this Virginia's first measles-associated death in 2026. A VDH spokesperson, Brookie Crawford, told WJLA it is the state's first recorded measles-associated death since 1978. To protect the family's privacy, VDH said it will not provide any further information regarding the death. Officials have not released whether the child had other health conditions, or the child's vaccination status, so this briefing does not infer a cause beyond what VDH stated: a child who tested positive for measles has died, in a death the department classified as measles-associated. Virginia has 220 measles cases in 2026, the highest since 1988 and more than the previous 34 years combined, WJLA reported from VDH; all 41 cases since Oct. 17 were in people who were unvaccinated or whose status was unknown. Nationally, CDC listed 5,838 confirmed cases as of Nov. 19 and 6 measles deaths, a figure it says is subject to change. ABC News, citing state health departments, said the Virginia death was the 12th measles-associated death reported by states this year, with other deaths reported in Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. Context: 2026 measles death reports The gap between CDC's 6 and the states' at least 12 is a difference in how deaths are counted, not a finding that any one official total is fabricated. In September, a CDC spokesperson said the agency would "report measles deaths if and when the National Center for Health Statistics reports measles as the underlying cause of death," a change from historically accepting state health-department reports, the KFF Health News Policy Watch (updated Oct. 15) and a Reuters explainer (Oct. 14, Nancy Lapid) documented. CDC's dashboard, updated Nov. 20, still lists 6 deaths for 2026 with a note that the number is subject to change, and says CDC is working with the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists on a standardized definition for deaths due to measles. Pennsylvania uses a "measles-associated death" definition: a death within 30 days of symptom onset, with clinical evidence and a positive lab test, that is not due to an unrelated cause such as a car crash (Pennsylvania Department of Health FAQ). The Philadelphia Inquirer reported on Nov. 18 that Pennsylvania had confirmed 8 measles-associated deaths, and that a Franklin County coroner's reported death had not been counted by the state because it did not meet that definition. Some deaths that states have counted involved people who also had other serious conditions; that does not, by itself, mean measles played no role. The Jefferson County, Pennsylvania, coroner's office told NBC News (Sept. 14) that an unvaccinated 40-year-old woman who died had asthma and COPD, was on oxygen around the clock, and tested positive for an active measles infection at the time of death. The Inquirer, citing local coroners and news reports, also described a Lancaster County woman in her 20s who had received cancer treatment. In one case, the official cause was later disputed. After Pennsylvania announced two "measles-associated" deaths in August, Lancaster County Coroner Dr. Stephen Diamantoni said a newborn who tested positive for measles died of a traumatic rupture of the spleen; he and the forensic pathologist said the spleen was not enlarged or inflamed and that the laceration was not related to measles (NBC News, Aug. 27; Lancaster Online, Aug. 27; WGAL, Aug. 30). The state health secretary at the time, Dr. Debra Bogen, still described the death as measles-associated, a term the department said applied when laboratory or epidemiologic evidence of measles is present. This briefing did not find an official state, CDC, or medical-examiner revision that withdrew a 2026 U.S. measles death as false. Claims that some 2025 Texas measles deaths were later "not measles" come from private reviews by Children's Health Defense, not from a state or CDC revision; CDC has continued to list those 2025 deaths, and they are outside this year's count.

Quick facts

  • VDH: a child age 0–4 who tested positive for measles has died; first measles-associated death in Virginia since 1978 (spokesperson to WJLA)
  • Officials have not released other health conditions or vaccination status
  • Virginia: 220 cases in 2026, highest since 1988 (VDH via WJLA)
  • U.S.: 5,838 confirmed cases as of Nov. 19 (CDC)
  • CDC lists 6 deaths for 2026 (subject to change); state health departments have reported at least 12 measles-associated deaths (ABC News, Washington Post)

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether this Virginia child had other health conditions, and the child's vaccination status (not released)
  • Whether CDC will add this death to its official count, and when
  • A complete, official national list reconciling every state-reported death with NCHS vital records
  • A final official cause-of-death determination in cases still under investigation, including any remaining questions in Pennsylvania

What happens next

  • VDH said its measles dashboard will next be updated Tuesday, Nov. 24, to reflect the death
  • CDC says it will update its death count as additional information becomes available and reviews are completed
  • CDC and CSTE are still developing a standardized case definition for deaths due to measles

U.S. · Strait of HormuzReported only

Three tankers are struck near the Strait of Hormuz

The United Kingdom's maritime security center, UKMTO, reported two tankers hit by unidentified projectiles early Saturday while leaving the Gulf near Khasab, Oman, after a Friday attack that set another tanker on fire, CNBC (from Reuters), Al Jazeera (with AFP and Reuters), and the Associated Press reported. No casualties were reported. TradeWinds said the three tanker incidents came in about 15 hours. Attribution to Iran is suspected, not confirmed. Iran's navy said a tanker caught fire after hitting a naval mine in a restricted zone, accusing it of ignoring warnings with its transponder off; it is not clear whether that is one of the UKMTO incidents. Al Jazeera's Tehran correspondent counted about 25 incidents in the past week. Before the war that began with U.S.–Israeli strikes in February, the strait carried nearly a fifth of the world's crude. The U.S. has been escorting tankers and blockading Iranian ports; those operations are the U.S. link in this briefing, not a confirmed role in Saturday's strikes.

Quick facts

  • Three tankers struck in about 15 hours near the Strait of Hormuz, off Khasab, Oman
  • UKMTO: two ships hit by unidentified projectiles Saturday after a Friday fire
  • No casualties were reported in the coverage reviewed
  • Attribution is suspected / apparent Iranian; Iran says one ship hit a mine
  • Before the war, the strait carried nearly a fifth of the world's crude

Not yet confirmed

  • Who fired, if anyone did; "suspected" and "apparent" are the terms used in the coverage reviewed
  • Whether Iran's mine claim describes one of the three UKMTO incidents, or a separate event
  • Any damage to oil flows or a confirmed move in crude prices from these specific strikes, beyond the general risk

What happens next

  • Further UKMTO or shipping-industry updates on the three ships
  • The U.S. escort and blockade posture in the Gulf remains in place
