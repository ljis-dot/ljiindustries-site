FAA Outage Update: two aviation outages in four days, officials say no cyberattack

This is an update on Friday's nationwide ground stop, which Briefed led with on Nov. 14, and on a separate United Airlines outage on Monday. The Federal Aviation Administration says a telecommunications failure, including work on a new router, flooded its network. In a questions-and-answers post, the F.A.A. said its investigation pointed to a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, where L3Harris was deploying a new router; those sites were blocked and the new-router broadcast was disabled. "Based on all available evidence, we have no indication that this was a cyberattack or an outside nefarious actor," the F.A.A. said. The stop ran from about 3:30 p.m. to 8:15 p.m. Eastern on Friday, Nov. 13, and about 3,500 airborne aircraft landed safely. Operators canceled about 12,462 flights that Friday, including 9,082 by major airlines; the F.A.A. said not all of those cancellations were caused by the ground stop. On Monday, Nov. 16, United Airlines said a technology issue delayed departing flights, with no impact on flights already in the air or on United Express, and that it had no indication it was a cybersecurity issue. The F.A.A. said that outage was a United technology problem, not an F.A.A. equipment issue. CNN, citing United spokesperson Leslie Scott, reported a disruption of about 30 minutes affecting fewer than 150 flights; the Associated Press described a longer disruption. The two events were not presented as connected. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has also said the Friday event did not appear to be a cyberattack.

Quick facts

  • Friday, Nov. 13: nationwide F.A.A. ground stop, about 3:30–8:15 p.m. ET
  • F.A.A.: a telecommunications failure, including work on a new router, flooded its network; no indication of a cyberattack
  • Monday, Nov. 16: a separate United Airlines systems outage delayed departures
  • United and the F.A.A. say the Monday outage was a United technology problem, not a cyberattack

Not yet confirmed

  • A final technical cause of Friday's failure, beyond the F.A.A.'s preliminary account of the router work and flooded network
  • A precise, official tally of how many Monday delays were caused by United's outage; CNN and the A.P. reported different durations

What happens next

  • The F.A.A. investigation into Friday's ground stop is continuing
  • Travelers should check with their airline for the latest on delayed or canceled flights

Nvidia reports $115.8 billion in revenue, up 103%, and guides to $130 billion

Nvidia reported fiscal third-quarter results Tuesday evening, its first earnings report since Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive. Revenue was $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier and 20% from the prior quarter, according to the company. Net income was $65.3 billion. Data-center revenue was $107.6 billion, up 110%. For the current quarter, Nvidia guided to $130.0 billion, plus or minus 3%, and said it assumed no China data-center compute sales. The company also raised its growth outlook for the following year to 75%. Board chair Harvey Jones said the board does not intend to reinstate Huang. Interim chief executive and chief financial officer Colette Kress remains in those roles. BBC reported that shares rose 4.8% after hours.

Quick facts

  • Q3 fiscal 2027 revenue: $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier (Nvidia)
  • This-quarter guide: $130.0 billion, plus or minus 3%; no China data-center compute assumed
  • Board chair Harvey Jones: the board will not reinstate Jensen Huang
  • Shares rose 4.8% after hours (BBC)

Not yet confirmed

  • How long Colette Kress will remain interim chief executive, and who the board will name as a permanent C.E.O.

What happens next

  • Investors will parse the call and the raised growth outlook
  • The board's C.E.O. search continues; it says it will not reinstate Huang

U.S. · ManilaReported only

U.S. and Thailand announce a substantial conclusion of a reciprocal trade agreement

In Manila on Tuesday, President Trump and Thailand's prime minister, Anutin Charnvirakul, announced a "substantial conclusion" of an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade, according to a White House joint statement. The statement said both governments "will now begin their respective necessary internal processes to prepare the ART for signature and entry into force." It is not a signed agreement and is not in force. The Hill also reported the announcement.

Quick facts

  • Announced Tuesday in Manila by President Trump and Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul
  • Described as a "substantial conclusion" of a reciprocal trade agreement (White House)
  • Both sides still have internal processes to finish before signature and entry into force
  • It is not a signed deal and is not in force

Not yet confirmed

  • The full text and detailed terms of the agreement
  • When the two sides will sign it and when it would enter into force

What happens next

  • Each government begins its internal processes to prepare the agreement for signature
  • Signature and entry into force would come after those steps

U.S. · AlaskaReported only

Mary Peltola wins Alaska's Senate race, giving Democrats a 56-44 majority

Democrat Mary Peltola has won Alaska's U.S. Senate race, the last 2026 Senate seat called, defeating two-term Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan. With about 99% of votes counted she had 50.1% of first-choice votes, so a ranked-choice tabulation was not needed, according to coverage of the Alaska Division of Elections count. The result projects a 56-44 Democratic majority in the Senate, including two independents who caucus with Democrats. NBC News called the race at 10:19 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday; the BBC also reported the result. Peltola would be the first Alaska Native U.S. senator and the first Democrat to win an Alaska Senate race since 2008. The incumbent's campaign has not conceded and said it would comment by the end of the week. A second candidate with a similar name, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., also appeared on the ballot. He is a retired elementary school teacher from Petersburg, Alaska, and he filed as a Republican under his legal name. Alaska Division of Elections Director Carol Beecher initially excluded him, concluding the filing was not a good-faith candidacy and was "filed with a purpose to confuse or mislead." He sued. Alaska Superior Court Judge Thomas A. Matthews ruled in June that the division had abused its discretion and relied on a "good-faith" requirement not in the state's constitution, statutes or regulations; the Alaska Supreme Court affirmed and ordered him onto the August primary ballot. He advanced from that primary. The New York Times reported in October that aides to Peltola's campaign and Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee personnel helped recruit him to siphon votes. Peltola said she knew nothing about other candidates on the ballot except herself and the incumbent and that she was "not interested in tricking voters." Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. has said he had no contact with her campaign — "Zero, none, zilch" — and that Sullivan is the name and party of his father and grandfather. Motives and any recruitment remain disputed. BBC Verify reported that even combining both Sullivans' first-choice votes would not have overtaken Peltola.

Quick facts

  • Peltola: 50.1% of first-choice votes with about 99% counted; last 2026 Senate seat called
  • Projected Senate: 56-44 Democratic majority, including two independents who caucus with Democrats
  • A second candidate, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired teacher from Petersburg, also ran
  • Officials first blocked him as meant to confuse voters; Alaska courts ordered him on the ballot
  • The New York Times reported people with Peltola's campaign helped recruit him; she said she knew nothing about it, and he has denied any contact with her campaign
  • The incumbent has not conceded

Not yet confirmed

  • Whether anyone in Peltola's campaign recruited the second Sullivan; the Times report is disputed by her denial and by his denial of any contact
  • Whether the incumbent will concede, or challenge the result, by the end of the week

What happens next

  • Certification of Alaska's Senate results
  • The incumbent's campaign said it would comment by the end of the week
  • Peltola is expected to be seated in the new Senate

World · Port-au-PrinceReported only

Haitians rally in support of Bukele and Salvadoran troops

Thousands of people marched Tuesday, Nov. 17, in Port-au-Prince and other cities in support of El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele and Salvadoran troops sent to Haiti to fight gangs, according to the Associated Press and Agence France-Presse. Al Jazeera, citing agency reporting, described crowds in several Haitian cities and, in one account, used the lower figure "hundreds." Signs included "Thank you, Bukele" and "Long live Bukele"; some marchers called for "drones and the dogs." In the afternoon in Port-au-Prince, police used tear gas as a march headed toward Sylvio-Cator stadium, which had recently been retaken. Bukele sent about 2,500 troops (A.P. and France 24 said some 2,500; A.F.P. has also said more than 2,000) after four Salvadoran aviation technicians went missing in an Oct. 18 ambush during a U.N.-backed Gang Suppression Force operation. The new surge is largely separate from that force: U.N. spokesman Stéphane Dujarric and later reporting have said only a few hundred of the Salvadoran personnel are under the U.N.-backed mission, with the rest operating under a bilateral Haiti–El Salvador arrangement. Haiti's government said Tuesday that its Council of Ministers approved a cooperation agreement; terms were not specified, and Salvadoran Vice President Félix Ulloa said Monday the bilateral agreement would not be made public. Salvadoran Security Minister Gustavo Villatoro said troops would leave once the missing airmen are found. Haitian Justice Minister Patrick Pélissier, speaking in San Salvador, said Tuesday there was "no date" for them to leave and that officials would work on a legal framework to stay longer, at least until elections next year. On the U.S. role, Axios reported that Bukele called President Trump and that Trump told him to "do what you have to do," citing anonymous sources. Deutsche Welle later quoted Trump on the record: "He said that he has a big problem with Haiti. I said: 'Look, you're running your country. You have to do what you have to do.'" Axios also reported that Bukele requested drones and transport and was officially denied, and that Trump said Bukele "doesn't need help." There has been no confirmed U.S. funding or U.S. troop role in this surge.

Why it matters to the US: President Trump has said he told El Salvador's president, Nayib Bukele, to 'do what you have to do' on Haiti; there is no confirmed U.S. funding or U.S. troop role in the surge.

Quick facts

  • Tuesday, Nov. 17: thousands marched in Port-au-Prince and other cities (A.P., A.F.P.)
  • About 2,500 Salvadoran troops, sent by President Bukele, are in Haiti to fight gangs
  • Most of the troops operate outside the U.N.-backed Gang Suppression Force
  • Haiti's government said it approved a cooperation agreement Tuesday; terms were not published
  • Trump has said, on the record, that he told Bukele he had to "do what you have to do"; no confirmed U.S. funding or U.S. troops in the surge

Not yet confirmed

  • A precise crowd size; Al Jazeera also used "hundreds," below the "thousands" figure from A.P. and A.F.P.
  • The unpublished terms of the Haiti–El Salvador cooperation agreement, and how long the troops will stay (Salvadoran and Haitian officials have given different accounts)
  • Any U.S. logistical support beyond Trump's public comment; requests for drones and transport were officially denied, per Axios

What happens next

  • The search for the four missing Salvadoran airmen continues
  • Haiti and El Salvador may publish, or keep private, the legal framework for the deployment
  • Whether the troops remain through Haiti's planned elections next year is unresolved
